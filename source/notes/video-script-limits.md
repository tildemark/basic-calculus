# Video Script: Basic Calculus - The Concept of Limits

**(Visual: Title slide "Calculus: The Concept of Limits")**

**[Intro]**
Hey everyone! Welcome to our guide on Basic Calculus. Today, we're talking about the "Why" of Calculus and the foundational concept of Limits.

**(Visual: Whiteboard showing: rate = 60 miles/hour, time = 2 hours. Distance = rate * time)**

**[Part 1: The Big Picture]**
Let's start with a quick comparison. Imagine you're driving, and your rate is 60 miles per hour, and your time is 2 hours. Using the formula "Distance equals rate times time", we multiply 60 by 2, cancel out the hours, and get 120 miles. 

This is Algebra. Algebra is for a STATIC world. It works perfectly when things stay exactly the same. 
But the real world doesn't work like that—things are always changing. That's where Calculus comes in. Calculus is the math of CHANGE.

**(Visual: Write LIMITS formula: lim (x→c) f(x) )**

**[Part 2: What is a Limit?]**
To do the math of change, we need a tool called the Limit. 
Here is the formula. We read this as: "The limit as x approaches c, of the function." It simply asks: as we get really, really close to a certain input, what is the output getting close to?

There are three main rules we're going to cover today.

**(Visual: "A. Substitution -> replace the variable x with a specified number")**

**[Part 3: Rule A - Substitution]**
The first rule is Substitution. This just means replacing the variable 'x' with a specified number. Let's run through some examples to refresh our basic algebra rules!

**Example 1:** The limit as x approaches 2, of (x + 3). 
Just substitute 2 for x. 2 plus 3 is 5. Done!

**(Visual: "Exponent Rule: x^2 = x * x")**

**Example 2 & 3:** Before we do the next one, remember the exponent rule: x-squared means x multiplied by itself. So negative 1 times negative 1 is positive 1.
If we have the limit as x approaches 5 of (2x), we substitute to get 2 times 5, which is 10.
What about the limit as x approaches -1 of (x-squared + 4)? We plug in -1. (-1) squared is 1. And 1 plus 4 is 5. 

**(Visual: PEMDAS reminder)**

**Example 4:** The limit as x approaches 3 of (x + 1) over 2. 
Remember PEMDAS—do the top part first like it's in parentheses. 3 plus 1 is 4. Then divide by 2. The answer is 2.

**(Visual: Roots reminder: 4, 9, 16, 25...)**

**Example 5:** The limit as x approaches 4 of the square root of x. 
Algebra rule reminder: a square root asks "what number times itself equals this?". The square root of 4 is 2!

**(Visual: Zero Rule: anything multiplied by zero is zero)**

**Example 6:** The limit as x approaches 0 of (5x-cubed + 2x + 7). 
Remember, anything multiplied by zero is zero! So 5 times 0-cubed is 0. 2 times 0 is 0. We're just left with 0 plus 0 plus 7... which is 7.

**(Visual: "B. Constant Rule")**

**[Part 4: Rule B - The Constant Rule]**
Moving on to the second rule: The Constant Rule. If there is no 'x', the limit is just the number itself.

**Example 7:** The limit as x approaches 10 of 42. 
There's nowhere to plug in the 10! So the answer is simply 42.

**(Visual: Show 0/0 trap)**

Now, look at **Example 8:** The limit as x approaches 3 of (x-squared - 9) over (x - 3).
If we use our substitution rule, we get 3-squared minus 9, which is 9 minus 9, or zero. On the bottom, 3 minus 3 is zero. 
We get zero over zero! You can't divide by zero. This leads us to our final rule.

**(Visual: "C. Zero in denominator rule -> factoring")**

**[Part 5: Rule C - Zero in Denominator (Factoring)]**
When you get zero over zero, you have to use factoring. 

**(Visual: Difference of squares: a^2 - b^2 = (a-b)(a+b) )**
Back to Example 8. We use the "Difference of Squares" rule on the top. (x-squared - 9) becomes (x - 3) times (x + 3). 
Now we have (x - 3) times (x + 3) all over (x - 3). 
Remember the Cancelling Rule: any number divided by itself is 1. So, we cancel out the (x - 3) on the top and bottom. 
We are left with just the limit of (x + 3). Substitute the 3, and 3 plus 3 is 6!

**(Visual: Example 9)**

**Example 9:** The limit as x approaches 2 of (x-squared - 4) over (x - 2). 
Again, difference of squares! The top becomes (x - 2) times (x + 2). The (x - 2) cancels out. We're left with x plus 2. Plug in the 2, and we get 4.

**(Visual: Example 10 and GCF)**

**Example 10:** The limit as x approaches 5 of (2x - 10) over (x - 5). 
Here we use another algebra rule: the Greatest Common Factor, or GCF. We can factor a 2 out of the top. So 2x minus 10 becomes 2 times (x - 5). 
The (x - 5) on the top and bottom cancel out. We're just left with 2!

**[Outro]**
And that's it! As you can see, solving basic limits is mostly about remembering your core algebra rules. Thanks for watching, and see you in the next one!
